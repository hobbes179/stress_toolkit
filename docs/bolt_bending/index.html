Bolt bending — shear and moment along the grip

Bearing from each plate is spread over its own thickness. Gaps and spacers carry no bearing, so they pass moment straight through.

    Method

    Everything below is reproducible with a calculator. The tool does no iteration and calls no solver — it evaluates closed-form expressions segment by segment.

    1  Model

    The bolt is treated as a straight beam whose axis x runs from the head bearing face (x = 0) to the nut face (x = L), where L is the total grip. The only transverse loads are the bearing pressures the plates apply to the shank, plus whatever the head and nut react. Bending is taken in a single plane, so the problem is one-dimensional.

    Each plate is assumed to bear uniformly over its own thickness. This is the conservative baseline: real bearing peaks toward the shear planes, which shortens the effective moment arm.

    2  Bearing intensity

    A plate of thickness ti carrying transverse load Pi applies a line load

    wi = Pi / ti   [lbf/in]

    over the span it occupies. Gaps and spacers get w = 0. They support nothing and simply carry shear across, which is why a spacer adds moment arm at no benefit. The shaded blocks in the joint elevation are these intensities, scaled to a common maximum.

    3  Equilibrium

    Force balance requires the plate loads to sum to zero:

    Σ Pi = 0

    Moment balance is a separate condition and is not automatic. Taking moments about the head face,

    Mres = Σ Pi · xi,   xi = x0,i + ti/2

    A symmetric double-shear stack gives Mres = 0 on its own. Any asymmetry — an offset spacer, unequal thicknesses — leaves a residue that a real bolt reacts through head and nut bearing. The tool closes it with an equal and opposite pair:

    RL = −Mres / L,   R0 = −RL

    applied at x = 0 and x = L. This adds no net force, and both diagrams then close at the nut. Unchecking the box drops the pair so you can see the raw imbalance.

    4  Integration

    Within a segment of constant w, starting at x0 with known V0 and M0, let u = x − x0:

    V(u) = V0 + w u
    M(u) = M0 + V0 u + ½ w u2

    Start with V = R0 and M = 0 at the head, walk the segments in order, and carry the end values of one into the next. Shear is piecewise linear; moment is piecewise quadratic. Add RL to the shear at the nut and it should return to zero.

    5  Locating the peak

    Peak moment sits where shear crosses zero. Inside a segment with w ≠ 0 that is

    u* = −V0 / w,   valid if 0 < u* < segment length

    Evaluate M at every u*, at every segment boundary, and at both ends, then take the largest magnitude. Nothing else needs checking — a quadratic has no other stationary points. Note that a gap has w = 0 and so contains no interior peak, but it is where the moment climbs fastest at constant slope.

    6  Section properties

    Round solid section of diameter d:

    Z = π d3 / 32,   A = π d2 / 4

    Use the thread minor diameter if threads or a thread runout fall near the peak moment. On long grips with spacers this is a common trap, because the peak often lands well inboard of where the shank ends.

    7  Stresses and margins

    fb = Mmax / Z,   fs = Vmax / A

    Bending is checked against a modulus of rupture, not against Ftu directly. A solid round has a fully plastic shape factor of 1.7; k = 1.5 is the usual defensible working value, and MMPDS bending MOR curves are better still where they exist.

    Fb = k Ftu
    MSb = Fb / (fb · FF) − 1
    MSs = Fsu / (fs · FF) − 1

    FF is the fitting factor. Shear here is the average V/A; the true peak on a round section is 4/3 of that, so add the factor yourself if your process demands it.

    8  Combined check

    Peak moment and peak shear almost never coincide, so pairing the two maxima is both wrong and needlessly harsh. The tool evaluates the interaction at every station and reports the worst:

    Rb = M(x)·FF / (Z Fb),   Rs = V(x)·FF / (A Fsu)
    MSc = 1 / √( max[ Rb2 + Rs2 ] ) − 1

    9  Worked example

    The default stack, by hand. Plates 1–3 at 0.250, 0.500 and 0.250 in carrying +1000, −2000 and +1000 lbf, with a 0.060 in spacer after plate 1. L = 1.060 in, so every plate runs at |w| = 4000 lbf/in.

    ΣP = 0. Moments about the head give Mres = 1000(0.125) − 2000(0.560) + 1000(0.935) = −60 in·lbf, hence RL = +56.60 and R0 = −56.60 lbf.

    Both diagrams close at the nut, which is the arithmetic check worth doing every time. If M(L) is not zero, the load split or the residual moment has been mishandled.

    10  What is not included

    No credit is taken for clamp-up, which genuinely stiffens the joint and reduces bolt bending but is hard to quantify by hand. No axial load, preload or prying. No bearing peaking: shortening the arm requires the Melcon & Hoblit treatment in AFFDL-TR-69-42, and the ESDU 91008 reduced arm is valid only at ultimate with plastic bearing redistribution, not where yield governs.

    The load split between shear planes is an input, not a result. It is statically indeterminate and should come from relative plate stiffness or a bounding sensitivity study. Plate bearing, shear-out, net section and lug strength are all separate checks.
